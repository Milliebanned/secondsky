01

Who loses money
on one wet day

Mines

Open-pit operators

Heavy rain turns haul roads to mud. Trucks slow or stop, and a planned blast can slip. A shift is lost, and the fleet's costs are not.

Building

Site managers

A concrete pour is booked days ahead: pump, trucks, crew. Rain on the day means cancelling, or risking a bad pour.

Farms

Field contractors

Harvest, spraying and hauling run on short windows. A wet day idles hired machines and crews.

02

What they do today

  1. Watch the forecast and absorb the loss

    Forecasts help plan, but they do not pay when they are wrong.

  2. Write weather days into contracts

    Time is extended, but the day's costs still land on someone.

  3. Buy weather insurance

    It exists, but it is usually written for a season or a large project, through a broker, and a claim needs evidence and someone to decide it.

What is missing is cover for one named day at one place, bought in minutes, that pays by rule from public records.

05

The honest limits

iSmall covers are expensive

The 2 USDC evidence fee is fixed. On a dry-season day with a 50 USDC payout, you pay several times the expected payout. It only makes sense when the payout is large compared with 2 USDC.

iiIt measures a grid cell, not your site

NASA POWER reports an area about 50 km across, and Open-Meteo is a modelled archive. A storm can miss the cell and hit your pit, or the other way round. That is why it takes two sources, and pays 50% when they split.

iiiThe two records often disagree

Over a year in 10 cities, on days when at least one source saw 10 mm, they split 64% of the time. See the study.

ivPayment is not same-day

NASA publishes a few days late, so a policy settles about 5 to 7 days after the day. Telegraph then answers in about 8 minutes.

vTestnet only

Sepolia, test USDC, payouts capped at 50. No real money, and no real customer yet.
