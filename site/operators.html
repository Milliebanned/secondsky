For the day that has to stay dry

Some work only happens on one day. If it rains, the crew still gets paid, the trucks still get paid, and the work moves. SecondSky pays out for that one day, decided by recorded rainfall, without a claim form.

Who loses money on one wet day

Open-pit mine operators

Heavy rain turns haul roads to mud. Trucks slow or stop, and a planned blast can slip. A shift is lost, and the fleet's costs are not.

Construction site managers

A concrete pour is booked days ahead: pump, trucks, crew. Rain on the day means cancelling or risking a bad pour.

Farm and field contractors

Harvest, spraying and hauling run on short windows. A wet day idles hired machines and crews.

What they do today

  1. Watch the forecast and absorb the loss. Forecasts help plan, but they do not pay when they are wrong.
  2. Write weather days into contracts. Time is extended, but the day's costs still land on someone.
  3. Buy weather insurance. It exists, but it is usually written for a season or a large project, through a broker, and a claim needs evidence and someone to decide it.

What is missing is cover for one named day at one place, bought in minutes, that pays by rule from public records.

Two independent records

Open-Meteo archive and NASA POWER, each bought from Telegraph by the contract itself.

Pays by rule

Both over the limit: 100%. They split: 50%. Neither: nothing. Nobody decides it by hand.

The honest limits

Small covers are expensive.The 2 USDC evidence fee is fixed. On a dry-season day with a 50 USDC payout, you pay several times the expected payout. It only makes sense when the payout is large compared with 2 USDC.
It measures a grid cell, not your site.NASA POWER reports an area about 50 km across, and Open-Meteo is a modelled archive. A storm can miss the cell and hit your pit, or the other way round. That is why it takes two sources, and pays 50% when they split.
The two sources often disagree.Over a year in 10 cities, on days when at least one source saw 10 mm, they split 64% of the time. See the study.
Payment is not same-day.NASA publishes a few days late, so a policy settles about 5 to 7 days after the day. Telegraph then answers in about 8 minutes.
Testnet only.Sepolia, test USDC, payouts capped at 50. No real money, and no real customer yet.

What I do not know yet

I have not spoken to a paying customer. These are the questions that decide whether this is a product:

  1. What does one lost day cost you, roughly: crew, machines, penalties?
  2. How many days a year does rain stop you?
  3. How far off can the rain record be before the payout feels wrong?
  4. Is a payout 5 to 7 days later fine, or does it need to be the same day?
  5. What would you pay for one day's cover?
