SecondSky — rain cover that asks two skies

Cover for one day that has to stay dry: a pour, a haul-road shift, a harvest. When the day is over, the contract buys that day's recorded rainfall from Telegraph twice, from two independent sources, and pays by rule. No one decides a claim by hand.

Every policy

Each bar is what one source said fell that day. The black line is the policy's rain limit. Every step links to its transaction and to Telegraph's own record of the job, which names the miner that answered.

What this is. Testnet, test USDC. Most policies so far were bought by our own test wallet to exercise settlement against real recorded weather (replays of past days, which cost more than they can pay). They are not customers, and are labelled "our test" below.

How often do two skies disagree?

We compared Open-Meteo and NASA POWER daily rainfall for 10 cities over a full year (Nov 2025 – Oct 2026, 3,650 city-days). On most days both are dry. But on days when at least one source reaches the limit, they often disagree:

A contract that trusts one source pays on that source's luck. Our first version refused to pay when they split and left it to a person, which on these numbers means most rainy days end in review. Version 2 pays half on a split, automatically, and prices that in: the premium uses 10 years of both sources at the exact spot. Method and code: scripts/agreement_study.py.

Who can do what

Anyone can settle a due policy, and finalize it a day after the answers arrive. The operator (a hot key on the quote server) signs prices up to a 50 USDC payout cap, finalizes at once after checking Telegraph's job records, and, if a job was answered by a miner other than the one named, can only send the policy back to be bought again. It cannot pick an outcome, move money or change settings. The owner (a cold key that never touches a server) controls the pool and sources. It can withdraw only money not promised to open policies. If a policy is never settled, the buyer can take the premium back after 7 days.
